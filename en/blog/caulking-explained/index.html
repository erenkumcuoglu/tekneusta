Boat Caulking Explained: How Long It Lasts, Methods and When to Renew

Why is caulking needed, when should it be renewed, and what are the modern methods? Essentials for wooden boat owners.

Boat caulking makes the seams between a wooden hull's planks watertight — traditionally with oakum and stopping, today often with a flexible polyurethane or polysulphide compound. Done well it lasts roughly 5–10 years; a leak that continues after the planks have swelled, or cracked and lifting stopping, means it is time to renew.

How long does caulking last?

On a well-maintained carvel hull, seams usually need local attention every 5–10 years, and a full reef-out and re-caulk is a 15–20-year job. What shortens it: long spells drying out on the hard, strong sun on the topsides, and hard or unsuitable seam compound that cannot follow the wood's movement.

Why is caulking needed?

Wood swells with moisture and shrinks as it dries. This movement opens and closes the seams. The caulking material must be flexible enough to accommodate it — holding water out without cracking.

Traditional and modern methods

The traditional method drives oakum (cotton/flax) into the seams and covers it with a stopping compound. Modern applications use flexible polyurethane/polysulphide sealants. The right method is chosen for the boat's construction and age.

When should it be renewed?

A boat that leaks, has lifting seams or dried, cracked stopping is due for caulking. Postponing small leaks can rot the wood beneath.

What affects the price?

Four things set the cost: total seam length (boat length and number of planks), how hard the old caulking is to reef out, the material chosen (oakum + stopping or flexible compound), and whether rot turns up under the seams once they are opened.
